# MASTER ART DIRECTION — NASA SPACE APPS 

## Core Visual Identity

Create an original, stylized 3D space exploration game with an art direction inspired by the visual simplicity, atmospheric environments, charming character proportions, and adventurous mood of the 2025 cooperative climbing game PEAK by Aggro Crab and Landfall.

The game should feel colorful, approachable, adventurous, slightly whimsical, and visually cohesive. It must look like a complete indie 3D game rather than a photorealistic space simulation or a generic asset-store prototype.

Capture PEAK's broad visual design principles without copying its specific character models, scenery, UI artwork, or other identifiable assets.

## 1. Geometry and Modeling

- Use low-to-moderate polygon 3D geometry.
- Build environments from large, readable, simplified shapes.
- Prefer chunky, organic silhouettes over tiny geometric details.
- Combine broad flat surfaces with subtle polygon facets.
- Use slightly irregular and rounded contours for natural formations.
- Avoid excessively sharp triangular terrain everywhere.
- Keep models lightweight and suitable for real-time rendering.
- Use simple primitive-based construction for smaller objects.
- Preserve a consistent level of geometric detail across all assets.
- Avoid photorealistic sculpting, intricate mechanical details, and excessive subdivisions.

## 2. Materials and Shading

- Use primarily matte materials.
- Prefer flat colors and gentle gradients over photographic textures.
- Use soft diffuse shading with restrained specular highlights.
- Introduce subtle color variation across terrain surfaces.
- Keep normal maps, roughness variation, and surface noise minimal.
- Use ambient occlusion sparingly to ground objects.
- Allow visible polygon facets where they improve shape readability.
- Avoid glossy plastic, hyperrealistic metals, complex PBR textures, and excessive reflections.
- Space equipment may use selective metallic and emissive materials while retaining the simplified visual language.

## 3. Color Direction

Use a cohesive, environment-specific palette with strong separation between characters, terrain, and interactable objects.

Each planetary environment should have:
- Two or three dominant environmental colors.
- Several muted secondary colors.
- One or two stronger accent colors for important gameplay elements.
- Soft lighting-driven color variation.
- Distinct colors for objects that players can interact with.

Suggested environments:

MARS:
Dusty orange, terracotta, warm sandstone, muted rust red, and soft beige.

MOON:
Cool gray, charcoal, pale silver, subdued blue-gray, and off-white.

ICY PLANETARY ENVIRONMENT:
Pale cyan, muted blue, lavender-gray, and icy white.

SPACE STATION:
Warm white, navy, desaturated gray, and small cyan or orange accents.

Avoid excessive neon colors, oversaturation, and random unrelated palettes.

## 4. Lighting and Atmosphere

Lighting should make simple geometry feel beautiful and expansive.

- Use soft directional lighting for primary illumination.
- Use gentle ambient lighting to prevent unnecessarily harsh shadows.
- Include subtle contact shadows.
- Use warm and cool color contrast where appropriate.
- Use atmospheric perspective on planets with atmospheres.
- Fade distant terrain into softer colors and lower contrast.
- Use environment-appropriate haze and fog.
- Preserve clear silhouettes of distant landmarks.
- Use cinematic sunrise, sunset, or diffuse daylight where appropriate.

IMPORTANT:
Maintain scientific plausibility. Airless environments such as the Moon should not contain atmospheric fog, Earth-like sky scattering, or weather effects.

Avoid heavy bloom, extreme contrast, excessive volumetric effects, and dramatic photorealistic lighting.

## 5. Character Art

Design original, friendly, stylized low-poly astronaut explorers.

- Use compact, slightly exaggerated body proportions.
- Make helmets relatively prominent.
- Use simplified torsos, arms, legs, boots, and gloves.
- Create clear silhouettes recognizable at a distance.
- Keep suit details large and readable.
- Use broad color blocks rather than intricate patterns.
- Minimize facial complexity.
- Use expressive body language and simple animation.
- Give characters distinctive but cohesive equipment designs.
- Use subtle secondary motion for backpacks and equipment.

Characters should feel charming and approachable without becoming overly childish.

Avoid photorealistic astronauts, detailed military-style equipment, and highly polished animated-film character rendering.

## 6. Environmental Art

Build large, readable, visually memorable landscapes using simplified terrain geometry.

Use:
- Broad cliffs and ridges.
- Large rock formations.
- Rounded or gently faceted boulders.
- Simplified craters.
- Layered hills and mountains.
- Strong silhouettes.
- Clear exploration routes.
- Distinctive landmarks.
- Sparse, purposeful environmental props.
- Open areas that communicate scale.

Create three visual depth layers:
1. Foreground: strongest contrast and clearest detail.
2. Middle ground: main exploration terrain and objectives.
3. Background: simplified distant landmarks with environment-appropriate depth treatment.

The environment should feel handcrafted and intentionally composed, even if procedural generation is used.

## 7. Scientific Equipment and Spacecraft

Apply the same simplified geometry to scientific and technological objects.

- Use chunky modular shapes.
- Favor recognizable silhouettes.
- Make important equipment visually distinct.
- Simplify buttons, panels, and mechanical components.
- Use selective glowing lights to communicate functionality.
- Avoid excessive exposed wires and complex mechanical details.
- Ensure instruments remain recognizable and scientifically appropriate to their function.

The equipment should feel believable but visually compatible with the stylized world.

## 8. Camera and Composition

Use a smooth third-person exploration camera unless gameplay requirements specify otherwise.

- Frame the player clearly against the environment.
- Use a moderate field of view.
- Keep important landmarks visible.
- Avoid excessive camera shake.
- Use smooth camera movement.
- Emphasize scale through large environments and small explorers.
- Maintain readable foreground and background separation.
- Keep the visual center of gameplay uncluttered.

## 9. UI and HUD

Create a minimal, cohesive game interface.

- Use clean, rounded panels.
- Prefer simple vector-like icons.
- Use restrained color accents.
- Keep HUD elements small and readable.
- Use subtle translucency where useful.
- Avoid cluttered menus and oversized sci-fi overlays.
- Clearly distinguish interactive objects.
- Provide readable scientific information when players inspect objects.
- Maintain accessible contrast and legible text.

The interface should support the stylized game world rather than visually overpower it.

## 10. Animation and Visual Effects

Use simple, expressive, slightly exaggerated animations.

- Friendly, readable character poses.
- Smooth transitions between movement states.
- Subtle body movement and anticipation.
- Lightweight particle effects.
- Restrained glow on technological elements.
- Small environmental effects appropriate to the planetary setting.
- Clear interaction feedback.
- Minimal screen-space distortion.

Animation should feel playful and responsive, without undermining the educational or scientific purpose.

## 11. Technical Implementation

Prioritize a coherent real-time 3D experience.

- Use reusable low-poly meshes.
- Prefer shared material palettes.
- Reuse modular environment pieces.
- Use simple lighting configurations.
- Keep draw calls and geometry complexity manageable.
- Use procedural terrain only when it preserves the intended artistic composition.
- Optimize assets for smooth real-time gameplay.
- Preserve visual consistency across all levels and environments.
- Use lightweight effects rather than expensive photorealistic rendering systems.

If the game runs in a browser, favor efficient WebGL-compatible rendering and maintain good performance on typical student laptops.

## 12. Overall Emotional Direction

The game should communicate:

- Curiosity
- Discovery
- Exploration
- Adventure
- Collaboration
- Scientific wonder
- Optimism
- A sense of scale

The world should feel inviting and visually memorable, with occasional moments of awe when players discover large planetary landscapes or important scientific locations.

## 13. Negative Art Direction

DO NOT create:
- Photorealistic graphics.
- Unreal Engine-style cinematic realism.
- Hyper-detailed rock textures.
- High-poly realistic character models.
- Smooth, glossy animated-film characters.
- Excessively angular terrain made entirely from obvious triangles.
- Pixel-art or voxel-art environments.
- Retro PS1-style pixelation.
- Heavy outlines around every object.
- Excessive bloom and neon lighting.
- Complex futuristic HUD overlays.
- Highly detailed mechanical surfaces.
- Unnecessary visual clutter.
- Inconsistent asset styles.
- Direct copies of PEAK's scouts, locations, or interface.

## Final Visual Target

The finished game should look like a charming, professionally art-directed low-poly 3D exploration adventure.

Its visual identity should combine PEAK-inspired environmental simplicity and atmosphere with original NASA-themed planetary landscapes, friendly astronaut characters, simplified scientific equipment, and an educational sense of discovery.

Prioritize cohesive art direction, readable gameplay, memorable environments, and strong visual atmosphere over raw graphical fidelity.

The result should feel like a real, polished indie exploration game—not a technical demo, photorealistic simulator, or collection of unrelated low-poly assets.
